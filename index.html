Stages of Grief

Stages of Grief is an exhibition that explores the range of ways artists represent loss, pain, and remembrance through their creativity. Curated by Marie Pacis, it features famous artists such as Frida Kahlo, Ilya Repin, Yayoi Kusama, and Vincent van Gogh, as well as Jozef Israëls, Kay WalkingStick, Maxim Vorobiev, and Patricia Belli.

Grief is often associated with depression, and it is a common assumption that it can be easily overcome with enough time and the right resources, but this does not consider the intense impact it can have on an individual. Through these works, this exhibition considers grief as an experience that does not follow a fixed or universal sequence. We examine how these artists transform difficult experiences into visual forms of emotions.

This production grows out of the curator’s personal experience of losing their mother at eleven years old. Inspired by Jozef Israëls’ The Last Breath, she became interested in examining the diverse ways artists represent grief. This primary work depicts a scene of mourning and innocence. Frida Kahlo’s The Wounded Deer expresses grief through symbolic transformation, representing herself as a human-deer hybrid that is injured by arrows. Sorrowing Old Man (At Eternity’s Gate) by Vincent van Gogh conveys emotional distress through the old man’s hunched posture and muted colors. Ilya Repin’s Ivan the Terrible and His Son Ivan focuses on portraying grief through guilt and remorse. Maxim Vorobiev’s Oak Fractured by Lightning uses the destruction of a tree as a metaphor for the sudden impact of losing someone. Altogether, these pieces demonstrate how some artists choose to represent grief through the human figure or symbolic imagery.

Other works in the exhibition extend beyond traditional art forms. Chandelier of Grief by Yayoi Kusama uses mirrors and repeated light to suggest that beauty and sadness can exist at the same time. The illumination that reflects across the room offers the idea that hope can still remain even in periods of darkness. Patricia Belli’s Maraña (Tangled) addresses ecological grief, bringing to light the fear of future generations that will face the consequences of climate change caused by pollution. Tears by Kay WalkingStick combines personal and collective forms of grief by incorporating the death of her husband and the historical pain experienced by Indigenous communities.

Through paintings and installations, Stages of Grief indicates that grief does not follow one predictable sequence or that it can be overcome within a specific amount of time. Instead, it is presented as a feeling in which you can experience sadness, anger, guilt, and hope, all at once.

By placing different forms of depicting grief alongside one another, the exhibition emphasizes how it is a shared human experience. It offers viewers reassurance, serving as a reminder that they are never alone in their grief, even if it feels isolating.

Jozef Israëls

The Last Breath

1872, Oil on canvas, 44 x 69 1/2 in

This is the main artwork that inspired the theme of this exhibition. The painting portrays a woman leaning over the body of a pale man, while an older woman tends to two children who are too young to understand the concept of death. I felt a personal connection to this piece because it reminded me of when I lost my mother. At the time, I was not that affected by her death, I continued to play my games and went along my life as if nothing happened. I witnessed everyone around me in tears, but the grief didn't get to me until years later.

Frida Kahlo

The Wounded Deer

1946, Oil on masonite, 8.8 x 12 in

This painting was created during the time Kahlo experienced difficulty in walking. She depicts herself as a half human-half animal hybrid, with lots of arrows piercing her. Kahlo expresses her grief not through a literal sense, but through symbolic imagery and references that represents the constant pain she endured.

Yayoi Kusama

Chandelier of Grief

2016, Steel, aluminum, one-way mirror, acrylic, chandelier, motor, plastic, LED

Installed in a dark room filled with infinity mirrors, the chandelier reflects repeatedly, creating an illusion of an endless stream of light. The work suggests that you can experience beauty and sadness at the same time, I interpret its light as a symbol of hope. Rather than depicting grief in a sorrowful way, Kusama's work indicates that moments of beauty can continue to exist even in the darkness.

Vincent Van Gogh

Sorrowing Old Man

1890, Oil on canvas, 2'7 x 2'1 in

Also known as At Eternity's Gate, this was created shortly before his death. This is a reference to a drawing called "Worn Out," a war veteran sitting with his head in his hands. Van Gogh associated the man's depressing expression to his belief with God. The figure's portrayal helps communicate the artist's emotional exhaustion.

Maxim Nikiforovich Vorobiev

Oak Fractured By Lightning

1842, Oil on canvas, 100 x 130 cm

On the surface, it appears to be an ordinary landscape painting, but this scene is an allegory of personal loss. This artwork was created as a response to the death of Vorobiev's wife, he indirectly communicates his grief through nature--showing the destructive and instability side to it.

Patricia Belli

Maraña (Tangle)

2024, Driftwood, plastic, animal bones, springs, cables

According to the art label, Belli created this installation in the wake of her sister's death from bone cancer. She also connects this work to her hopelessness over protecting future generations from the effects of climate change. Belli demonstrates how grief can also extend beyond the individual by combining ecological grief with personal mourning.

Ilya Repin

Ivan The Terrible and His Son Ivan

1883-1885, Oil on canvas, 78.5 x 100 in

Unlike the other artworks which are based more on the artist's experience, this presents grief through a historical narrative. Based on the traditional account that Ivan the Terrible struck his son during an argument, the painting focuses more on the aftermath rather than the violence. His wide-eyed expression, the desperation and remorse in his embrace as he holds his dying son. Through this work, Repin reminds us that grief is inseparable from guilt and regret.

Kay WalkingStick

Tears

1990, Deer hide, cow hide, copper, wood, feathers, bone, beads, corn, stones, turquoise, and Anasazi potsherds

Modeled after a funerary scaffold, this piece combines personal grief, as well as reflecting on the genocide that Indigenous people experienced in "the area that we now call the United States." She connects the work to the death of her husband by using a shirt he made for him as a bundle that sits on top of the scaffold. WalkingStick shows how personal and historical grief can overlap.
